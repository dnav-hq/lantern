The word door

Rung two of the deep dive, and the one that can do harm. Every verse below is real BSB text, fetched live. The word is hebel (H1892), the brief’s own worked example, and it was chosen because it makes the whole argument by itself.

Why this word proves the rule. The BSB renders hebel as futility, futile, a breath, a vapor and fleeting — all below, all real. The lexicon’s single gloss is “vanity”, a word the BSB does not use in any of them. Hand a reader “vanity” as the meaning and you have taught them to misread every verse on this page. Show them the verses and the range is obvious without anyone asserting anything. That is the entire feature.

1. The door

Reading order is the argument. The verse first, then how it is rendered here, then the neighbourhood, then the occurrences. The gloss is near the BOTTOM with its provenance attached, which is the opposite of every lexicon tool.

A lexicon gloss
vanity
Brief entry, TBESH. One editor’s one-word summary — not a definition, and not what the BSB chose in any verse on this page.
What this can and cannot tell you. You are looking at where a word is used and how it has been put into English. That is evidence about the word’s range, not a definition of it, and a word does not carry all of its uses into any one verse. What it means here is settled by this sentence, not by this list.

2. The occurrences, which are the point

Rule R1: occurrences appear in their sentences, never as a gloss list. The verse you came from is pinned at the top so you do not lose your place. All real BSB text.

Paging: flat and chronological, not grouped by rendering. Grouping occurrences under their English renderings would quietly rebuild the ranked sense list that R2 forbids — it would read as “here are the meanings, with examples”. Canonical order keeps them as evidence rather than as a taxonomy. Twenty at a time.

3. The thing that changed since the brief was written

The footnotes door already owns the dotted underline. It shipped on 2026-09-01. If a salient word is also marked with a dotted underline, the reader meets two different doors wearing one costume — and they lead somewhere genuinely different (a translator’s alternative versus a word’s neighbourhood). The brief predates this and does not account for it. Options, on the real verse:

A · nothing “Futility of futilities,” says the Teacher
B · dotted “Futility of futilities,” says the Teacher — collides with footnotes
C · tint “Futility of futilities,” says the Teacher — collides with the reader’s own highlights
D · baseline rule “Futility of futilities,” says the Teacher

My recommendation: A, with D held in reserve. A means the word door is not marked in the verse at all — it is reached from the Study side, or by tapping a word deliberately, and the reading page gains no new furniture. That protects the thing the whole product is built to protect: a chapter that still reads like scripture. It also sidesteps the collision entirely rather than negotiating it.

D is the fallback if A proves undiscoverable: a solid 2px baseline rule reads as a different object from a 1px dotted line, and colour stays out of it. B is out. C is out — tint means “I marked this” and the reader’s own marks must win.

4. Two questions I cannot answer for you

Is “how the BSB renders it, with counts” allowed? It is the most useful row on the page and it is the one that could smuggle the ban back in. R2 forbids ranking SENSES. Counting how one translation team put a word into English is a fact about the BSB, not a claim about Hebrew — and it is the honest antidote to the lexicon’s “vanity”. But a reader may still read the biggest number as the real meaning. I think it stays, labelled exactly as it is (“How the BSB renders it”, never “meanings”), but you should decide that knowingly rather than inherit it from me.

How much grammar does a non-specialist want? The door above says “Masculine singular noun”. The data can say “Qal perfect third person masculine singular” for verbs, which is accurate and opaque. My recommendation: plain words, and only what changes the reading — tense, number, whether it is a command. The full parse can sit behind the provenance line for anyone who wants it. Lantern is for someone who reads carefully, not someone who has had a year of Hebrew.
