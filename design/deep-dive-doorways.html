The deep dive as one system

One verse, one entrance, three doors, one way deeper. Every word, count, rendering, coordinate and verse on this page is read out of the shipped bundles — public/bible/words/**, public/bible/bsb.json.gz and public/map/places.json.gz — not invented. The note is docs/proposals/deep-dive-doorways.md.

The rule this whole page exists to show. One entrance under a verse the reader chose. Each door asks one question and its glance is one screen that answers it — no scrolling required to get the answer. Everything else sits behind one quiet fold, in the same words on every door. Three doors that obey that read as one deep dive; three doors that each dump their whole layer read as three features.

Why Genesis 12:3. It is the one verse where all three doors are genuinely full, measured rather than assumed. Word: bāraḵ (H1288) stands three times in it and 330 times in the Old Testament, put into English 141 different ways. Connections: the New Testament quotes it twice by name (Galatians 3:8, Acts 3:25) and Genesis repeats the promise to Isaac and Jacob. Map: Genesis 12 carries 8 geocoded places — Haran to Egypt — the chapter is a journey. Ecclesiastes 1:2 was the other candidate and it fails the test: its word door is the best in the Bible (hebel, already drawn in design/word-door.html), but Ecclesiastes 1 has one place in the whole chapter (Jerusalem) and the verse has no quotation of it anywhere. One verse cannot show a system with one and a half doors.

It does not fight the selection bar, because it is not doing the bar's job. The bar is fixed to the bottom of the screen and answers do something to this verse — write on it, mark it. The doorways row is in the text, under the verse it belongs to, and answers go into this verse. Two registers, two places, no third button in the bar. It is also exactly where the shipped word-door line already sits (WordDoor.tsx's “The words behind this verse”), so this is that line generalised rather than new furniture.

Each door is a question plus the smallest fact, never the answer. “3× here”, “2 quote · 4 echo”, “Haran → Egypt” — enough to make you curious, not enough to satisfy you. That is the information-gap finding in deep-dive-study.md's addendum: complete ignorance produces no curiosity, and a filled gap produces no tap.

Never an empty door, shown rather than claimed. On Ecclesiastes 1:2 this same row renders two doors — “The word behind ‘futility’ · 4× here” and “Where Scripture picks this up · 1 echo” — and no map door at all, because Ecclesiastes 1 has one place in the entire chapter and none in that verse. The row is built from what the bundles actually hold for this verse, so a thin verse gets a thin row and a plain verse gets no row.

The footnotes door is not in this row, on purpose. It shipped 2026-09-01 and it owns the dotted underline inside the sentence, because a translator's alternative belongs on the words it is about. Repeating it as a doorway would give one door two costumes.

The one thing this glance changes about the shipped door. Today the door opens on a word chooser and the reader must pick before anything is answered. The doorway already named the word, so the door opens on it; the verse's other five content words wait under the fold. One question asked, one question answered.

Four things moved to make it end on one screen, and the arithmetic is the argument. The same content laid out as the shipped door is 1,220px on this screen — a screen and a half, which is the complaint. The rendering chips went under the fold (the count is one sentence and says the same thing); the pinned repeat of the verse went, because the verse is the door's heading 200px above it; the word chooser went; and occurrence rows show a two-line window of their sentence rather than all six lines of Genesis 1:28. That is 807px, and nothing was deleted — every piece is one tap away.

The window must be centred on the word, not on the start of the verse. Here “blessed” happens to fall in the first line of all three occurrences, so a plain clamp looks right. It will not be, in general: clamp around the tapped word and lead with an ellipsis when the window starts mid-sentence. A window that hides the word is a bare reference wearing a sentence's clothes, which is exactly what R1 forbids.

All three of the verse's own occurrences are tinted, not just one. One Hebrew word wears three English coats inside a single sentence — “I will bless”, “bless”, “will be blessed”. That is the anti-single-meaning argument made on the reader's own verse, for free, without ranking or asserting anything. The shipped door tints one instance; this is a change.

There is no root or family line, and that is a measured decision, not an omission. A lemma entry in the shipped index carries lemma, transliteration, morph class, gloss, Greek sense text, renderings and occurrences — no relation to any other lemma. There is no family data to show. Guardrail R5 also keeps etymology out of the door entirely, so if a family line is ever added it must be a link to another word's occurrences, never a story about where this one came from.

The reason it connects is the content, not decoration. “Quotes it” and “said again, to Jacob” are why the reader would tap; a percentage or a filled rail is not. The connections prototype (design/reference-deep-dive.html) proved the meter was unintuitive and that order plus prominence carries relevance perfectly well — that finding is kept and the rail is gone.

Why this door is built first. It is verse-anchored and translation-independent: its data needs no per-word alignment, so it works on the BSB, the KJV and the NET alike, and it never has to say “the deep dive works on the BSB”. It is also the door whose glance is naturally one screen, which makes it the cheapest place to prove the rule.

Framing is the whole feature. The shipped map (MapView.tsx) opens on the entire Bible world and 1,335 places; a reader on Genesis 12 then has to find Haran themselves. Here the viewBox is computed from this chapter's own coordinates, so the chapter reads as a journey and the world it sits in is still visible behind it.

The faded layer needs thinning at this zoom, and that is a finding. 161 of the map's 1,335 places fall inside this frame, and drawn all together at chapter zoom they are a grey smudge over Judea rather than context. This artboard draws every third one; the real answer is thinning by prominence or clustering, and it is a small piece of work the map door needs that the whole-world map did not.

Disagreement is drawn, not hidden. Bethel and Ai come through the data as disputed — three and five rival candidate locations, the best of each scoring 716 and 522 against OpenBible's own 1,000-point scale — and a hollow ring says so on the map and in the list. No competitor bothers; it is the cheapest possible expression of “the reader decides”.

Two honest problems this artboard exposes, both in the data rather than the design. Shechem and the Oak of Moreh carry the identical coordinate, and Bethel and Ai sit half a pixel apart at this frame — so labels must collide-resolve or merge (here: one label for Shechem, leader lines for Bethel and Ai). And a region — Canaan, Egypt, the Negev — arrives as a single point, so Egypt is a dot in the Delta and Canaan a dot north of Shechem. Drawing a region as a dot quietly asserts something false. The note proposes what to do about it.

No route line. The chapter does move Haran → Shechem → Bethel → the Negev → Egypt, and drawing that arc would be beautiful, but the order comes from reading the chapter, not from the geodata, and a drawn route claims a path nobody recorded. The verse numbers in the list carry the same information honestly.

Its grammar here
Verb · Nifal · perfect · third person common plural “will be blessed”

Conjunctive waw | Verb - Nifal - Conjunctive perfect - third person common plural. The other two instances in this verse are Piel: “I will bless” (imperfect cohortative, first person singular) and “those who bless you” (participle, masculine plural construct). Same word, three forms, one sentence.

What this can and cannot tell you. You are looking at where a word stands and how it has been put into English. That is evidence about the word's range, not a definition of it, and a word does not carry all of its uses into any one verse. What it means here is settled by this sentence.

The long tail: paging, and here is why not the other two. 109 chips is a wall, so twenty show and the rest page — the same control the occurrence list already uses, so the door has one paging idiom rather than two. A bare cap (“top 20, the rest hidden”) loses the fact that most of the tail exists at all, and the tail is the evidence that the range is wide. A search box is worse than both: a reader who can type “vanity” or “curse” into a rendering list will find the meaning they already expected, which is confirmation wearing the costume of research.

Order is load-bearing. Grammar, then renderings, then the gloss — and the gloss carries no heading weight, no larger type, no colour. R2 and R3 are obeyed by placement, not by a disclaimer: put a one-word gloss at the top of a screen and nothing written underneath it will be read.

What deeper means for the other two doors is described in docs/proposals/deep-dive-doorways.md rather than drawn — the note's “Deeper, for the other two doors” section. In short: the connections door's deeper layer is more of the same list (the remaining references, plus the thematic ones, plus the stacked follow-a-connection behaviour the prototype already proved), and the map door's deeper layer is the map itself — free pan and zoom out to the whole Bible world, the rival candidates for a disputed place, and every verse a place appears in.
